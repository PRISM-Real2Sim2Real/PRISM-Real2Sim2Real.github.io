REAL ROBOT · ZERO-SHOT TRANSFER

Teaching robots how to pick up different things

Humanoids are starting to walk.
Now, let’s give them something to pick up.

To be useful in our homes, a robot needs to handle more than one carefully placed object. It needs to approach a box from the side, reach down for a ball, or find a hold on an unfamiliar bucket. A change in shape, size, or orientation can demand a different way of moving.

People make these adjustments almost without thinking. We bring a lifetime of watching, reaching, lifting, and trying again. How can we give humanoids a richer set of experiences, too?

02 / The data bottleneck

A sea of videos.
A shortage of the right ones.

A promising answer is visual imitation: show the robot how a person does it. Work such as VideoMimic demonstrates how human videos can become contextual whole-body skills. But scaling this approach brings a practical question: where do the right demonstrations come from?

01

Motion capture

Accurate movement data, but specialized equipment and capture sessions make collection cumbersome to scale.

02

Internet video

Abundant, but made for people to watch. An ordinary, uninterrupted view of someone picking up a household object is rarely the main attraction.

03

Record it yourself

A useful way to teach the skills you care about. Yet filming every object, arrangement, and scene becomes a growing collection effort.

03 / Our solution

Start with one demonstration.
Imagine what else could happen.

PRISM starts with a real video of a relevant motion. We use video-to-video generation, or V2V, to turn that example into new interactions: change the object, and let the person’s movement adapt to it.

THE RECORDED EXAMPLECOUNTERFACTUAL POSSIBILITIES
One grounded motion. New objects and new ways of interacting.
COUNTERFACTUAL /

An interaction that didn’t happen in the original recording, but could have happened if the object had been different.

In this work, we focus on changing objects—their geometry, size, and configuration—and the motions that go with them. The broader idea could also extend to different scenes; that is a direction for expansion, rather than a result demonstrated here.

Why start from video instead of text?

A text prompt must describe an entire event. A reference video already supplies the scene layout, a visible person, and the rough sequence of the task. V2V can build on this structure while changing the interaction. That grounding makes it easier to ask for useful variations, although generated motion still needs to be checked and made physically plausible.

Instead of filming every variation, we amplify a small set of real recordings into 256 counterfactual videos for training.

05 / From video to robot

An imagined interaction.
A physical skill.

Using these videos, PRISM builds demonstrations a humanoid can learn from. A convincing video is only the beginning: generation and reconstruction can both introduce errors, and a human’s body is different from a robot’s.

The connection through the pipeline is contact—where the hands should meet the object, and how that relationship should be maintained as it moves.

  1. 01

    Reconstruct the interaction

    Recover the person, the scene, and the object’s shape and motion in a shared 3D world. Contact anchors help keep them consistent, even when parts are occluded.

  2. 02

    Retarget to the humanoid

    Adapt the motion to the robot’s body while preserving the intended hand–object contacts and seeking physically plausible trajectories.

  3. 03

    Learn in simulation

    Train a teacher to co-track the robot and object. Distill these demonstrations into one policy that uses onboard depth observations.

  4. 04

    Transfer to the real world

    Deploy the policy without real-world fine-tuning. The robot picks up, carries, and drops unfamiliar objects, with high-level steering commands.
